Regulatory setbacks and cautious institutions

Perp DEX

Weekly theme | Singapore’s MAS adds Hyperliquid to its Investor Alert List

Event: On June 26, MAS added the Hyperliquid website and Hyper Foundation to its Investor Alert List alongside offshore venues including KuCoin, Bitget and Bybit, which was added on June 17. In Australia, ASIC extended a crypto licensing application deadline to September 30.

Mechanism: The list warns consumers about entities that may be mistaken for MAS-regulated firms. Listing is distinct from an enforcement ban, and users could still access the platform. Local banks and payment providers could nevertheless restrict fiat access. Hyperliquid said it had never applied for an MAS licence and described its role as permissionless infrastructure.

Second-order effects: The case extends regulatory attention from centralized exchanges to onchain venues. Japan’s FSA action involving Bybit in 2024 offers a point of comparison. If local institutions also restrict payment routes, a warning can have consequences beyond its formal legal status.

JPMorgan sees limited institutional demand for perpetuals and links about half of onchain volume to funding from 12 wallets

Event: In a June 29 report, JPMorgan's derivatives team said it found little or no genuine institutional demand for onchain perpetuals and attributed most trading to retail speculation and market making. Citing Hyperliquid data, the team said just 12 wallets accounted for roughly half its perpetuals volume.

Mechanism: The team identified three barriers for institutions: the absence of a forward curve for pricing term hedges, the lack of physical delivery, and open-ended basis risk from funding payments. Features convenient for retail users can be difficult to accommodate in an institutional risk framework.

Second-order effects: The concentration of half the volume in 12 wallets raises questions about liquidity during liquidations. The finding also echoes CME's argument in its regulatory suit that perpetuals primarily serve retail speculation. This is the fourth related development in the dispute tracked since June 19.

Aster's 99% fee-funded buybacks and matching burns cover about 2.94 million ASTER in 12 days, with AOS-1 listing fees joining the pool

Event: From June 17 to 29, Aster earmarked 99% of daily trading fees to buy ASTER in the market for the veASTER reward pool and burn a matching team allocation. The first round covered about 2.94 million tokens. AOS-1, introduced June 26, lets tokens already listed on Binance Spot or Alpha apply for listing for 50,000 USDT. Listing fees also fund future buybacks. Aster aims to reduce total supply from 8 billion to 3 billion.

Mechanism: Aster allocates 99% of trading fees to buybacks. Repurchased tokens reward veASTER stakers, encouraging holders to lock up their tokens. The AOS-1 listing fee contributes a second source of cash to the same pool, linking expansion of the market list to the supply policy.

Second-order effects: Lower volume during deleveraging means less fee income and smaller buybacks, amplifying the effect of a slowdown. Aster's daily volume was roughly 2–5% of Hyperliquid's. Earmarking 99% of fees for purchases at that scale shows how aggressively mid-sized venues are competing through tokenholder distributions.
